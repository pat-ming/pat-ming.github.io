Mitigating Capability Degredation in LLM Self-Improvement Via Representation Geometry Regularization

October 12th, 2026

Jiaqi Tian, Ting Long · arXiv 2026

OPSD can be shown to display capability degredation over time. That is, problems that the student model would've normally gotten right at the beginning of training begin to become more "difficult" as the number of training epochs increase. This is a failure mode that many people are aware of but its not one whose direct root cause is easily identifiable. The authors perform a theoreitcal study and identify potential causes: 1). When viewing the hidden representations of the entire sequence at the final layer before logits, there is clear clustering where intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster borders are clear, and 2). The entropy between the student and teacher do not change very much. Motivated by this, the authors introduce Representation Geometry Regularization (basically adding in another loss term).

\begin{gather*} \mathbb R^{T\times d}\ni H = [\textbf{h}_1,...,\textbf{h}_T], \mathbb R^{T\times d}\ni H_\text{ref} = [\textbf{h}^\text{ref}_1,...,\textbf{h}^\text{ref}_T] \\ \mathcal L_\text{geo} = \|HH^\top-H_\text{ref}H^\top_\text{ref}\|_F^2, \quad \mathcal L_\text{ent} = \|H^\top H - H_\text{ref}^\top H_\text{ref}\|_F^2 \\ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_\text{OPSD} + \alpha \mathcal{L}_\text{geo} + \beta\mathcal{L}_\text{ent} \end{gather*}
“RGR effectively reduces correct-to-incorrect transitions while maintaining the self-improvement capability of OPSD”

Recursive Self-Improvement via On-Policy Distillation for Reasoning

September 28th, 2026

Shangjian Yin, Zehao Zhao, Kavosh Asadi, Rui Liu, Yuchen Lu, Shike Mei, Hang Cui, Luke Simon, Zhouxing Shi, Hamed Firooz · arXiv 2026

OPSD removes the external teacher by using a frozen copy of the student that gets to see the ground-truth solution. The problem this paper finds is that the frozen teacher is a bad guide on wrong trajectories: even knowing the answer, it puts 94.5% on EOS at the end of an incorrect response and only 22% on reflection tokens like “Wait.” Essentially, it would rather stop than tell the student to rethink. The fix (DCE) is to refresh the teacher from the updated student every round, so it is the same weights with a different context and a stop-gradient. Heuristically this makes sense, since a teacher stuck at step 0 can’t teach a revision habit it never picked up. On Qwen3-8B the average goes from 30.35 (OPSD) to 65.76. SRCL then trains on shorter verified rewrites to keep the length down, and it collapses without DCE, so it isn’t doing the real work. What I’m not sold on: the base model with thinking mode on already gets 62.08 at about the same token count, and at 1.7B it beats this method outright (36.88 v.s. 26.88). So is this self-improvement, or is it teaching the non-thinking model to think? Responses go from 4K to 19K tokens, which looks a lot like the length growth in OPSA. It also partly answers my question of how far this can iterate. Not that far. Accuracy peaks around step 100 and the 1.7B run falls apart after that.

\[ \mathcal{L}_G^{(k)} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,g),\; y^{(k)} \sim p_{\theta_k}(\cdot \mid x)} \left[\frac{1}{m} \sum_{t=1}^{m} \Delta\left(q_{k,t},\, p_{k,t}\right)\right], \qquad q_{k,t} = \operatorname{stopgrad}\left[\, p_{\theta_k}\left(\cdot \mid C_t^{\text{prev}}(x, g, \tau, \tilde{y}^{(k)}_{\lt t})\right)\right] \]
“Frozen teacher favors termination over reflection.”

Rethinking On-Policy Distillation of Large Language Models: Phenomenology, Mechanism, and Recipe

September 27th, 2026

Yaxuan Li, Yuxin Zuo, Bingxiang He, Jinqian Zhang, Chaojun Xiao, Cheng Qian, Tianyu Yu, Huan-ang Gao, Wenkai Yang, Zhiyuan Liu, Ning Ding · arXiv 2026

The failure mode that got me to read this: a stronger teacher can completely fail to improve a student while a weaker one succeeds. The paper says two conditions decide whether OPD works. First, the student and teacher need compatible thinking patterns. Heuristically this makes sense, since if the teacher thinks differently, a large proportion of the distillation signal goes to speaking style and not capability. Second, a higher score doesn’t mean new knowledge. Don’t have to think too hard about this one. A teacher needs to know something the student hasn’t already seen. The reverse distillation experiment is my favorite part: distill an RL-trained 1.5B model toward its own pre-RL checkpoint and it regresses right back, and a 7B teacher from the same family drags it to the same place. So OPD is ostensibly copying thinking patterns, not scores. Mechanically, the gains come from the tokens in both models’ top-k (the overlap ratio goes from 72% to 91% in a successful run), and training on only those matches full OPD. I’m not sure how to square that with OPSA, which says the low-logp tokens are the ones that matter. The part that worries me most is the end: the teacher’s reward gets worse the deeper into the trajectory you go, which is a bad sign for long-horizon work.

\[ \mathcal{M}_{\text{overlap}} = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\frac{\left|S_t^{(p)} \cap S_t^{(q)}\right|}{k}\right], \qquad S_t^{(p)} = \operatorname{TopK}(p_t, k),\quad S_t^{(q)} = \operatorname{TopK}(q_t, k) \]
“Higher scores ≠ new knowledge.”

How Is AI Actually Producing New Mathematics? (verifiers and pushing reasoning)

September 14th, 2026

Reading across Tsoukalas et al. (arXiv:2605.22763), Feng, Trinh, Bingham et al. (arXiv:2601.22401), Novikov et al. (arXiv:2506.13131), and Tzachristas, Tzachristas & Sui (ACL 2026) · a talk I put together for HINT Lab

This week I stepped back from training tricks and tried to answer a simpler question: when an AI “solves” an open problem, who actually decides it counts? The honest answer is that it’s always a verifier, and the kind of verifier you pick determines both what you can reach and how it fails. The formal-proof-search paper is the clean case: the verifier is the Lean type-checker, so correctness is exact, and they resolve 9 of 353 open Erdős problems (4 genuinely novel) plus 44 of 492 OEIS conjectures. What struck me is that the dumbest loop — a basic agent alternating generation with Lean — replicates the Erdős wins; the fancy evolutionary search only pays off in cost on the two hardest instances. The verifier is doing the real work, not the search. The failure mode is the formalization gap: a mis-stated theorem still compiles, and they had to amend “density” to “lower density” in their own inputs after the agent found proofs.

When the verifier is a person instead (the Gemini Deep Think study on 700 “open” Erdős problems), the funnel is brutal: 700 attempted → 212 flagged plausible → 27 after non-expert filtering → 13 meaningfully correct → 4 novel, and even that count was “once as high as 9” before literature search knocked it down. The expensive step turns out not to be proving anything — it’s checking whether the result already exists. Half the “solutions” were rediscoveries, including one sitting in an offhand 1981 remark for 44 years. And when the verifier is just a score (AlphaEvolve), it’s the cheapest of the three and gives you the 4×4 complex matrix multiplication in 48 scalar products, the first beat on Strassen in 56 years — but it only reaches problems whose answer is a constructible object you can benchmark, and benchmarks get gamed under search pressure. The thread I kept pulling on: in all three, the model will sometimes quietly answer a slightly different question than the one asked, and whether that gets caught is entirely a property of the verifier, not the model.

“The strongest results are those where verification is cheap relative to generation.”

Does On-Policy Distillation Really Distill? From Noisy Teacher to Self-Improvement

September 1st, 2026

Yi Ding, Ruqi Zhang · arXiv 2026

In OPD the teacher grades trajectories that are off-policy for it, so this paper asks whether that supervision is even reliable. It isn’t. For a 4B teacher about 30% of the answer-token signals point the wrong way, and it gets worse as the teacher gets bigger. The surprising part is that the student doesn’t care: training on only the noisy trajectories, only the clean ones, or both all land in about the same place. So, where do the gains come from? Ostensibly from suppressing the low-logp tokens the student sampled. Throw away the top 80% of tokens by logp, replace every advantage with a fixed −0.5, and you still match OPD with no teacher at all. Heuristically this makes sense to me, since a token the student is already confident in has basically no gradient left. OPSA then scales the negative advantage with entropy and beats OPD by a lot (48.85 v.s. 32.08 Avg@32 on AIME24 with Qwen3-1.7B). I find this a little unsettling next to OPDVR, where the sign of the teacher signal clearly mattered. The noise here is only defined on the boxed answer tokens though, so it may not be the same split. It also raises the question of how far this can iterate, since it’s only redistributing mass the student already has.

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{OPSA}} = -\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|S_{\text{lowest20}}|} \sum_{i \in S_{\text{lowest20}}} A_i^{\text{dyn}} \log \pi_\theta(y_i \mid x;\, y_{\lt i})\right], \qquad A_i^{\text{dyn}} = -\frac{1}{2} - \frac{H_i - H_{\min}}{2\,(H_{\max} - H_{\min})} \]
“…an operation that requires no teacher at all.”

On-policy Distillation with Verifiable Reward

August 31st, 2026

Wenze Lin, Jiale Zhao, Xitai Jiang, Songde Rao, Yining Li, Shenzhi Wang, Bingxiang He, Gao Huang · arXiv 2026

RLVR grades the trajectory, so the reward is correct but sparse. OPD grades every token, so the reward is dense, but nothing in the loss cares whether the answer was right. This paper combines the two with a single ReLU and no new hyperparameters, which I honestly didn’t expect to be enough. The verifier picks the sign of the per-token reward and the teacher-student log-ratio picks the magnitude. Heuristically this makes sense to me: if the teacher is more confident in a token and the trajectory ended up correct, it was likely a good choice, so reward it. If the student was the confident one and the answer was wrong, punish it. Everything else gets no feedback, which turns out to be about half the tokens. That part surprised me. The gains over standard OPD are small (47.8 to 49.1 average on Qwen3-4B), so I’m not fully sold yet, but I like how little machinery it needs. It also raises the question of whether the thrown-away tokens were ever doing anything, which is where the next paper I read picks up.

\[ R_{\text{OPDVR}}(o_t) = \begin{cases} \max\left(0,\ \log \dfrac{\pi_T(o_t \mid q, o_{\lt t})}{\pi_\theta(o_t \mid q, o_{\lt t})}\right) \cdot (+1), & \text{trajectory correct,} \\[1.2em] \max\left(0,\ \log \dfrac{\pi_\theta(o_t \mid q, o_{\lt t})}{\pi_T(o_t \mid q, o_{\lt t})}\right) \cdot (-1), & \text{trajectory incorrect.} \end{cases} \]
“… Combining them is a promising direction: OPD supplies dense supervisory signals, while RLVR provides task-level correctness”
